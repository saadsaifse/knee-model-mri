Why cycling hurt after a while

Per pedal stroke, cycling is easy on the kneecap. With a well-set saddle the hardest push happens at about 90° of bend, where the kneecap sits deep in its groove and presses on its largest contact patch. The model puts the outer-facet peak near 3.3 MPa, against about 11 MPa per running stride. The kneecap also hardly shifts outward, because it never gets near the shallow end of the groove.

So why did it start to hurt after some distance? Three things add up:

  • The count. At 85 rpm each knee does about 5,100 strokes an hour. Each one is gentle, but an irritable facet and two irritated tendons respond to the total, and that pain typically arrives late in a ride or after it.
  • The set-up. A saddle a few cm too low, or too far forward, plus a big gear or a climb at low cadence, means deeper bends and more torque per stroke. Toggle it in Fig 2: the peak rises to about 5.3 MPa, 60% more on every stroke. This is the classic cause of pain at the front of the knee in cyclists.
  • Knee drift. As the hip muscles tire, the knee starts to track inward at the top of the stroke. Cleats rotated wrongly, or too little float, force the same thing. On the bench that is the anchor slide moving outward: more outward pull on the kneecap.
Bike check. At the bottom of the stroke, with your heel level, the knee should stay 25–35° bent, never straight and never well bent. Keep cadence at 85–95 rpm and change down early on climbs. Your cleats should let the heel sit naturally (a little float helps), and your knee should travel straight up and down over the pedal, not inward. Done like that, cycling is one of the best ways to keep your fitness up while running load comes down.
