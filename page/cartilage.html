The cartilage crack: is it dangerous, and how does it heal?

The report says "a tiny linear focal full-thickness cartilage denuding and underlying subchondral bony edema overlying the lateral patellar facet". In plain words: in one small spot, a hairline line of the cartilage on the back of your kneecap has worn through to the bone, and the bone just under it is irritated, which shows up as bright "edema" on MRI. Around it the cartilage is mildly softened (chondrosis) in a few places.

Is it dangerous?
Not in an urgent sense. Small cartilage lesions like this are common on runners' MRIs, including in people with no pain, and many stay the same size for years. The real risk is slow enlargement if the same spot keeps getting overloaded. With a kneecap that rides outward, that spot is exactly where the load goes. So the goal is to calm that spot down and spread its load, not to fix the line itself.
Will it heal?
Honestly: the cartilage itself won't regrow to normal. Adult joint cartilage has no blood supply, so a full-thickness line doesn't knit back like bone or tendon. It may partly fill with tougher scar cartilage, or simply stay as it is. What does heal is the irritated bone underneath, and that's usually the main source of pain. It settles over weeks to a few months once its load is under its tolerance. Your falling pain fits that.
So what does "cured" look like?
A quiet knee. The line still shows on a scan, but it doesn't hurt, doesn't swell, and doesn't grow. You get there by lowering peak stress on the outer facet: stronger quads and hips, better tracking, a quicker running cadence, and no sudden jumps in load. Gentle, repeated loading also helps the cartilage, because it gets its nutrients from being squeezed and released. That's why cycling with a correct saddle and the exercises here are good for it, while long rest isn't.
Do I need more than exercise?
Usually not, for a lesion this small. Injections such as PRP or hyaluronic acid have mixed evidence and aren't first-line. Surgery (cartilage repair, or moving the tendon attachment to fix the tracking) is for larger lesions, repeated dislocations, or a TT-TG well above 20 mm, after months of rehab have failed. None of those describe your report. Supplements (collagen, glucosamine) are low-risk but have weak evidence.
Warning signs
The knee swells after runs (puffy, tight, hard to fully bend). It truly locks or catches and won't straighten. Pain is worse each week rather than better, or wakes you at night. Any of these means the lesion is unhappy: see your orthopaedic doctor, who may repeat the MRI.
