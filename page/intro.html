Your left knee: from MRI to marathon

The good news comes first. Nothing inside the knee is torn or loose: the menisci, ligaments, the cartilage of the main joint, bone and fluid are all normal. Every finding is about the kneecap and its two tendons: how the kneecap runs in its groove, and how hard it presses on the way.

Lateral subluxation, 0.8 cm
With the leg straight and relaxed in the scanner, your kneecap sat about 8 mm toward the outside of the groove it should run in.
TT-TG 1.7 cm
The bump on your shin where the patellar tendon attaches sits 17 mm to the outside of the groove's centre. That's the upper limit of normal (above ~20 mm is called abnormal). Every time your thigh muscle pulls, this offset tugs the kneecap outward.
Trace chondromalacia, lateral facet
The cartilage on the back of the kneecap is mildly softened in several spots, with one tiny full-thickness crack and some bone irritation under it, all on the outer facet. That's where an outward-riding kneecap presses hardest.
Quadriceps and patellar tendinosis
Both tendons are mildly irritated and thickened from load, without any tear. This is common in runners and responds to progressive loading.
Medial meniscus extrusion, small Baker's cyst
The inner meniscus bulges slightly past the joint edge without a tear. The small fluid pocket behind the knee is usually harmless and often follows joint irritation.

Put together, this pattern is usually called patellofemoral pain with maltracking. The bench below is a working model of it: the kneecap is a sled in a groove, pulled by the quadriceps and tied to the shin. It runs walking, running, stairs and cycling, travels to 120° of bend, and shows what rehab can and can't change. The rest of the page runs the same model through a running stride, your bike, the home exercises and the gym, then covers the cartilage crack and the December marathon.
